Attention-based neural networks (e.g., Transformers and related architectures) currently achieve state-of-the-art performance across language, vision, and multimodal tasks \cite{vaswani2017attention,brown2020gpt3,openai2023gpt4,dosovitskiy2021vit,radford2021clip}. Despite their empirical success, we still lack reliable local explanations for why a given model produced a particular output, how internal mechanisms associated with ``reasoning-like'' behavior arise during training, and how we can steer models toward desired behaviors with minimal side-effects. 

Following the Kautz taxonomy \cite{kautz2022thirdaisummer} we are able to build systems that leverage trained models and chain their processing to achieve impressive goals. Those systems mitigate the various shortcomings of current language models, for instance.

This thesis studies local interpretability in attention-based models through three interconnected lenses: (i) training dynamics, with emphasis on abrupt transitions such as late generalization (grokking-like phenomena); (ii) behavioral control, focusing on neuro-symbolic steering methods that enforce human-interpretable constraints; and (iii) robustness diagnostics, investigating whether internal activation/attention signatures can reveal distribution shifts or adversarial perturbations before output quality visibly degrades. 

The core hypothesis is that high-level behaviors emerge from partially identifiable internal structures (features/circuits) that can be tracked across training and inference. Building on this hypothesis, we test whether logic-inspired constraints can target and modulate such structures in a controllable way, and whether the same internal signals can serve as early-warning indicators for shifts and attacks. 

Concretely, the thesis will
(1) characterize representational changes around grokking-like transitions using mechanistic probes;
(2) implement and evaluate a constraint-based steering pipeline (neuro-symbolic validators and reranking/decoding integration) and measure locality/regressions; and
(3) design detection criteria for shifts/attacks based on internal patterns rather than outputs alone.
The expected outcome is a coherent framework and empirical evidence connecting training-phase emergence, controllable steering, and diagnostic signals under distribution shift.

\section{Research questions and hypotheses}
To make the proposal directly testable, we organize the core claims into three research questions and paired hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Emergence):} Can distribution shifts induce measurable late-generalization transitions that coincide with stable changes in internal sparse representations?
    \item \textbf{H1:} In controlled settings for grokking, late test improvements are preceded or accompanied by increases in the consistency of cross-seed/cross-layer features, as suggested by previous results on induced grokking under distribution shift \cite{carvalho2025inducing_grokking_distribution_shifts}.

    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Steering locality):} Can logic-guided steering change targeted behavior while keeping collateral regressions bounded?
    \item \textbf{H2:} Logic-based constraints applied over sparse/interpretable internal features can improve rule satisfaction with an explicit and acceptable quality trade-off, measurable through task quality versus constraint-adherence curves \cite{Garcez2009,serafini2016ltn}.

    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Diagnostics):} Are internal sparse-feature signatures more reliable for perturbation/shift detection than output-only heuristics?
    \item \textbf{H3:} Sparse latent signatures provide competitive or better detection stability (ROC-AUC/PR-AUC and calibration) than output-only baselines, consistent with prior ViT/CLIP evidence on stego and FGSM/PGD perturbations \cite{carvalho2026interpretable_stego_detection_sae}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Proposal positioning}
\label{sec:proposal-positioning}
\textbf{Thesis statement.} This proposal argues that local interpretability for attention-based models can be made operationally useful when sparse feature representations, cross-layer alignment, and logic-based validators are evaluated jointly under explicit behavioral and mechanistic criteria.

\textbf{Novelty beyond prior publications.} The accepted grokking work and the SAE-based perturbation diagnostics establish feasibility in two isolated tracks. The thesis contribution is to integrate these tracks with a steering-locality program in one unified protocol, so that emergence, controllability, and robustness diagnostics are analyzed with shared objects ($h_\ell$, $s_\ell$, $T_{\ell\to k}$), common evaluation logic, and explicit cross-track ablations.

\textbf{Evidence status.} \emph{Completed:} controlled late-generalization evidence and initial sparse-feature diagnostics. \emph{Partial:} steering-controller implementation and preliminary logic-guided experiments. \emph{Pending:} finalized steering protocol, full baseline/ablation matrix across all goals, and transfer/failure characterization under the frozen evaluation plan.

\section{Intrinsic limitation of interpretability methods}
Most interpretability methods face a tension between plausibility and faithfulness: explanations that are intuitive (e.g., natural-language rationales) may not be causally grounded, while mechanistic methods  (e.g., probing, attribution, feature discovery) may be difficult to summarize \cite{jacovi2020faithfulness}.
%Additionally, many methods are sensitive to model scale, prompt distribution, and representation drift across layers.
In addition, modern models are highly distributed: internal variables are often polysemantic, drift across layers, and change across training regimes and prompts. 
These limitations motivate local, \emph{testable criteria and evaluation protocols that link internal measurements to externally verifiable behavioral effects}.

\section{Analogy to biology}
Neural networks resemble biological systems in the sense that complex organization emerges from simple learning rules, and the resulting structure is not explicitly designed.
Like biological systems, neural models exhibit emergent organization that is not explicitly programmed by designers. 
This analogy is useful as a methodological stance: progress comes from proposing falsifiable hypotheses about internal organization (e.g., stable features or circuits) and testing them through interventions and controlled evaluations, rather than relying solely on post-hoc narratives.
We adopt the stance that progress comes from measurable, falsifiable hypotheses about internal structure, e.g., tracking stable features/circuits---rather than relying solely on post-hoc narratives.
