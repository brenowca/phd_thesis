\section*{Notation and model setup}
This chapter establishes the minimum notation and model setup used throughout the thesis to discuss sparse auto-encoders (SAEs), cross-layer transcoders, and logic-guided steering. We avoid a tutorial treatment of Transformers and focus instead on the representational objects we measure and manipulate.

We denote a tokenized input sequence by $x_{1:T}$ and the model dimension by $d$. A Transformer with $L$ layers produces a residual stream $h_\ell \in R^{T\times d}$ after each layer $\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}$. Each layer applies attention and an MLP that write updates into the residual stream; we do not require head-level detail beyond noting that attention heads operate position-wise and share the residual pathway.

Unless otherwise stated, we collect activations as follows: $h_\ell$ denotes the residual stream immediately after layer $\ell$; optional probes may target head outputs or the MLP pre-/post-nonlinearity, but all core analyses use $h_\ell$. SAEs operate on $h_\ell$ to produce a sparse code $s_\ell = E_\ell(h_\ell)$ and a reconstruction $\hat h_\ell = D_\ell(s_\ell)$. We evaluate reconstruction error, sparsity of $s_\ell$, and stability across seeds/epochs.

To compare or transfer representations across depth, we define a cross-layer transcoder $T_{\ell\to k}: s_\ell \mapsto \hat s_k$, which aims to align layer-$\ell$ features with the feature basis learned at layer $k$. Logic validators provide soft scores for predicates/rules evaluated on $s_\ell$ or $\hat s_k$. Steering refers to using these scores to influence generation via logit adjustment, constrained decoding, or reranking.

\textbf{Implications for this thesis:} The symbols $h_\ell$, $s_\ell$, and $T_{\ell\to k}$ are the canonical objects for measurement and control. All metrics (reconstruction, sparsity, stability, alignment, constraint satisfaction) are reported with respect to these objects. The steering surfaces considered later act on logits using signals derived from validators applied to $s_\ell$ or $\hat s_k$.

\section{Core deep learning concepts used in this thesis}
\label{sec:core-dl-concepts}
This section summarizes only the deep learning concepts directly required by the proposal claims on emergence, steering locality, and diagnostics. We avoid broad historical coverage and focus on concepts that define measurable quantities used later in the methodology and ablation design.

\subsection{Representation learning and distributed features}
In high-capacity neural models, useful concepts are usually encoded in distributed form rather than in one-neuron-one-concept form. A single latent concept can span multiple directions, and one coordinate can carry multiple overlapping factors. For interpretability, this means we should model feature geometry explicitly and evaluate whether discovered features are stable and reusable across runs and layers, instead of assuming semantic atomicity from raw coordinates.

Superposition and polysemanticity are therefore expected behaviors at scale, not pathologies that disappear by default \cite{anthropic2022superposition,anthropic2023monosemanticity}. In this proposal, sparse dictionaries are used as an operational tool to reparameterize activations into a more analysis-friendly basis, not as a claim that the model is ``truly sparse'' in an ontological sense.

A thesis-native example is the case where two checkpoints show similar task behavior but yield different raw activation bases; if a sparse dictionary and matching protocol recover corresponding features, we treat this as evidence of underlying representational continuity rather than mere coordinate coincidence. \textit{Takeaway for this thesis:} evidence supporting C1/C2/C3 from this concept is based on reconstruction fidelity, sparsity level, and cross-seed/epoch feature correspondence.

\subsection{Generalization, overfitting, implicit regularization}
We model training as minimization of empirical risk under regularization:
\[
\min_{\theta}\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}(f_{\theta}(x_i), y_i) + \lambda\Omega(\theta).
\]
In modern overparameterized settings, good generalization can emerge despite near-zero training error due to optimization bias, data structure, and architecture priors. This is central for interpreting delayed generalization phenomena and for avoiding output-only explanations of behavior shifts \cite{kaplan2020scalinglaws,hoffmann2022chinchilla}.

Operationally, we track train/test behavior jointly over time windows (pre-transition, transition, post-transition), rather than using a single endpoint score. A compact statistic used throughout this thesis is the time-indexed generalization gap:
\[
\mathrm{gap}(t)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{test}}(t)-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}(t).
\]
In grokking-like settings, delayed improvement corresponds to a late contraction of this gap after training performance has already saturated.

A thesis-native example is a run where training loss is nearly flat while test accuracy improves later; this is interpreted as representational reorganization only if internal measurements (e.g., feature stability/alignment) change coherently in the same phase window. \textit{Takeaway for this thesis:} C1 claims require joint behavioral-mechanistic evidence, and C3 diagnostics must beat output-only heuristics that rely only on post-hoc performance signals.

\subsection{Optimization dynamics and phase transitions}
Training can exhibit phase-like behavior: fast fitting of easy patterns, slower reorganization of internal representations, and delayed behavioral transitions. This perspective motivates phase-aware checkpoints and multi-metric reporting, especially in grokking-like settings where test behavior changes long after training metrics appear saturated.

For mechanistic interpretation, the key risk is false positives from curve-only narratives: an apparent transition in accuracy may reflect evaluation noise, calibration effects, or metric saturation rather than a meaningful internal change. Therefore, phase boundaries are treated as analysis windows where both behavioral and representational diagnostics must be examined.

A thesis-native example is reduction of feature drift around the same interval where delayed generalization emerges; this co-occurrence supports the hypothesis that internal organization is changing, while a transition without such internal evidence is treated as insufficient for a mechanistic claim. \textit{Takeaway for this thesis:} C1 acceptance requires behavioral shift and mechanistic shift to co-occur under the same experimental protocol.

\textbf{Implications for this thesis:}
C1 (\emph{Emergence}) is tested through phase-aware representation dynamics, combining delayed behavioral transitions with feature stability/alignment changes over matched training windows.

C2 (\emph{Steering locality}) is tested through constrained interventions where behavioral gains must be accompanied by bounded collateral changes in internal and external metrics.

C3 (\emph{Diagnostics}) is tested by internal-signal detectors that must outperform or match output-only baselines under calibration and transfer checks.

\section{Attention-based model internals relevant to interpretation}
\label{sec:attention-internals}
\subsection{Self-attention as content-based routing}
Self-attention computes token-to-token mixing weights from query-key compatibility and applies them to values:
\[
\mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V.
\]
For interpretation, attention is treated as a routing mechanism that redistributes information across positions; its relevance is not only in attribution maps but in how it modifies downstream residual activations where features are measured \cite{vaswani2017attention,olsson2022inductionheads}. \textit{Takeaway for this thesis:} attention is analyzed through its effect on $h_\ell$ and derived sparse features, not as a standalone explanation artifact.

\subsection{Residual stream as computation substrate}
At layer $\ell$, we use a residual-style update view:
\[
h_{\ell+1} = h_{\ell} + \Delta^{\text{attn}}_{\ell}(h_{\ell}) + \Delta^{\text{mlp}}_{\ell}(h_{\ell}).
\]
This representation makes cross-layer analysis operational: SAEs are trained on $h_\ell$, and transcoders map between sparse bases inferred from these layerwise states.
\textit{Takeaway for this thesis:} the residual stream is the primary substrate linking mechanistic analysis and controllable intervention.

\subsection{MLP blocks, feature superposition, and polysemanticity}
MLP blocks can be interpreted as content-dependent transformations over residual features; in practice they often exhibit superposition, where multiple latent factors share coordinates \cite{geva2021ffn,anthropic2022superposition}. This motivates sparse dictionary methods and explicit stability tests across seeds/epochs.
\textit{Takeaway for this thesis:} polysemanticity is treated as a measurable risk and SAE training/ablation is the primary mitigation mechanism.

\subsection{LayerNorm and training stability (why it matters for feature comparability)}
Normalization layers affect activation scale and distribution, which can change apparent feature sparsity and cross-run comparability even when task behavior is similar. Therefore, normalization-aware reporting (e.g., consistent hook points and scale-sensitive diagnostics) is necessary when comparing features across checkpoints, layers, or architectures.
\textit{Takeaway for this thesis:} feature-level conclusions are accepted only under controlled activation collection and comparable normalization conditions.

\section{Decoder-only transformer variants and invariants}
\label{sec:decoder-variants-invariants}
\subsection{Baseline decoder-only stack (GPT-style)}
Decoder-only models combine autoregressive prediction with stacked attention-MLP blocks and a residual stream, which is the baseline setting for this proposal's interpretability objects \cite{radford2018improving,radford2019language,brown2020gpt3}. What changes with scale is mainly capability and optimization behavior; what remains invariant is the layerwise representation interface used by $h_\ell$, $s_\ell$, and $T_{\ell\to k}$.
\textit{Takeaway for this thesis:} GPT-style decoders define the reference analysis interface for all later variants.

\subsection{MoE routing variants}
MoE models replace dense MLP computation with expert routing, increasing conditional computation efficiency \cite{fedus2022switch,jiang2024mixtral}. The main challenge is routing-induced feature instability; however, residual activations and layerwise sparse coding remain analyzable with stricter variance controls.
\textit{Takeaway for this thesis:} MoE changes transfer difficulty, not the validity of representation-level measurement.

\subsection{Long-context adaptations}
Long-context improvements modify efficiency and context handling, often without removing the same core residual-attention interface \cite{rae2022scaling,press2022alibi}. This can alter distribution of activation norms and long-range dependencies, which should be treated as a shift in analysis conditions rather than a new interpretability regime.
\textit{Takeaway for this thesis:} long-context variants are handled as controlled setting shifts with unchanged core measurement objects.

\subsection{Instruction/alignment adaptations}
Instruction tuning and preference optimization alter objective functions and behavior priors while keeping architecture mostly fixed \cite{ouyang2022instructgpt,bai2022constitutional,wei2022chainofthought,rafailov2023dpo}. This is directly relevant for steering claims because improved instruction behavior may mask or reshape internal mechanisms if not measured with mechanistic metrics.
\textit{Takeaway for this thesis:} alignment-stage behavior changes must be evaluated jointly with internal feature and validator metrics.

\section{Architecture lineage from ChatGPT and why mechanistic interpretability still transfers}
\label{sec:arch-lineage-chatgpt}
From a systems perspective, ChatGPT can be read as a product layer built on top of decoder-only Transformer language models, instruction tuning, and preference optimization procedures such as RLHF \cite{vaswani2017attention,ouyang2022instructgpt,christiano2017rlhf,openai2023gpt4}. In this sense, the practical jump from base next-token predictors to useful assistants reflects training and alignment choices over a stable architectural backbone rather than a complete departure from Transformer internals.

Tracing backwards, the core lineage is well defined: Transformer blocks \cite{vaswani2017attention}, GPT-style autoregressive pre-training \cite{radford2018improving,radford2019language}, large-scale in-context learning behavior \cite{brown2020gpt3}, and instruction-following alignment pipelines \cite{ouyang2022instructgpt}. This progression is the same history often emphasized in build-from-scratch perspectives for modern reasoning-capable models \cite{raschka2024buildllm}: capability gains accumulate through scale, data, and training objectives while preserving key computational primitives.

Forward and parallel variants relevant to this thesis retain these primitives while changing operational details. Open-weight families (e.g., LLaMA-style models) keep decoder-only residual-stream computation \cite{touvron2023llama}; mixture-of-experts variants introduce sparse routing over MLP submodules while preserving layered attention-residual composition \cite{fedus2022switch,jiang2024mixtral}; and long-context model engineering largely modifies efficiency and context handling without removing layerwise residual features as analysis targets \cite{rae2022scaling}. Reasoning-oriented adaptations also predominantly alter training curricula, supervision formats, or inference-time search, again operating over the same internal representational objects.

For this thesis, the relevant invariants are explicit: residual streams, attention-mediated token interactions, MLP-channel transformations, and depth-wise composition. These invariants justify using common measurement objects across variants, namely $h_\ell$ (layer activations), sparse feature dictionaries $s_\ell$, and cross-layer mappings $T_{\ell\to k}$.

\section{Mechanistic interpretability under architectural variation}
\label{sec:mechinterp-variation}
Architectural variation changes important details: tokenizer granularity, model scale, optimizer and data mixture, context length, and (in MoE settings) routing sparsity. These changes affect interpretability difficulty, especially feature stability and cross-run comparability, but they do not eliminate mechanistic observables. Layer activations remain available, sparse dictionaries can still be trained, and cross-layer correspondence can still be tested via explicit alignment criteria.

This motivates a transfer principle used throughout the proposal: interpretability claims should be attached to measurable objects that are architecture-robust. In our pipeline, these are $h_\ell$, $s_\ell$, $\hat s_k$, and $T_{\ell\to k}$, paired with reconstruction, sparsity, stability, alignment, and validator satisfaction metrics. The same principle supports cross-variant comparability: we do not assume feature identities are identical across models, only that comparable measurement protocols can detect when local structure is stable, transferable, or fragile.

The approach has boundaries. Transfer can degrade when routing is extremely sparse and highly input-conditional (making feature identity unstable), when multimodal fusion introduces non-homogeneous representational spaces, or when aggressive compression/distillation/quantization collapses fine-grained internal geometry. For this reason, negative transfer outcomes are treated as first-class results, and claims are restricted to settings where protocol-level validity is empirically demonstrated.

\subsection*{Research gap and thesis positioning}
\label{sec:research-gap-positioning}
Most prior work either presents architecture history or evaluates a specific interpretability method in isolation. The missing piece is an integrated argument about why and when local mechanistic measurements should transfer across modern model variants. This thesis addresses that gap with a unified, measurable framework centered on sparse features, cross-layer alignment, and logic-based semantic validation. Success and failure are operationalized in the methodology chapter through explicit metrics, baselines, and falsification criteria (Chapter~\ref{chpt:circuitry}).

\section{Learning phenomena}
We briefly position the learning phenomena relevant to this work. Generalization denotes performance on unseen data; overfitting occurs when training performance improves while validation/test performance degrades; underfitting is failure to fit even the training data. In practice, we monitor paired training/validation curves and compare gaps over time to assess whether improvements reflect memorization or genuine structure acquisition.

Modern deep models often benefit from implicit regularization (e.g., optimization bias and architectural priors), which can reconcile high capacity with good generalization. Training dynamics can exhibit phases: rapid memorization, representation re-organization, and late convergence. Of particular interest is late generalization or ``grokking'': after the training loss saturates near zero, the test performance remains low for a long period and then sharply improves, stabilizing near training performance.

For our purposes, we treat grokking as a measurable delay between the end of the memorization phase and the onset of generalization, observable on controlled synthetic tasks. We track this delay alongside mechanistic signals such as the stability of sparse features $s_\ell$ and their reconstruction quality. We distinguish grokking from double descent by its temporal pattern (delayed improvement at near-zero training loss) and its association with representation re-organization rather than model capacity sweeps.

We use these phenomena in two ways: as motivation to collect activations at multiple training phases and as a source of experimental conditions that stress representation alignment across layers.

\textbf{Implications for this thesis:} Chapter~\ref{chpt:learning_phenomena} details datasets and measurements for late generalization. Here we commit to (i) reporting stability/drift of $s_\ell$ across epochs/seeds; (ii) selecting training windows that include pre-/post-generalization phases; and (iii) using synthetic settings to elicit representation changes that test the robustness of SAEs and transcoders.

\section{Grokking: Background and Related Work}
Grokking has been reported across multiple datasets and architectures, with early evidence coming from algorithmic tasks where labels are generated by a known rule (e.g., modular arithmetic) \cite{Grokking}. The defining signature is a long delay between training convergence and a later, sharp improvement in test performance. This delay suggests that late generalization is not just about reaching low training loss, but also about the emergence of useful internal structure after a period of apparent stagnation.

\citeonline{liu2022Grokking} present a detailed study of how hyperparameters influence grokking in an MLP trained on a reduced MNIST training set, but they do not analyze which properties of the data distribution make grokking more or less likely. In contrast, this thesis focuses on identifying data conditions that induce or prevent grokking and how those conditions interact with representation alignment across depth.

Mechanistic analyses of grokking in specific architectures provide complementary insights. \citeonline{nanda2023GrokkingProgress} reverse-engineer one-layer transformers trained on modular arithmetic and track component-level progress using spectral tools; their work motivates the use of phase-specific measurements during training. \citeonline{vzunkovivc2022grokkingPhase} model grokking as a phase transition in rule learning and connect it to statistical properties of the teacher model. \citeonline{levi2023grokkingLinear} show that grokking can occur even in linear models, strengthening the case that data distribution, not model complexity alone, can drive delayed generalization. \citeonline{kumar2024grokking} propose that grokking corresponds to a transition from lazy to rich training, with evidence from kernel regression and controlled neural settings.

Several works frame grokking in terms of competing circuits or broader learning dynamics. \citeonline{varma2023explaining} propose a circuit efficiency hypothesis in which a fast memorizing circuit is eventually overtaken by a slower, more efficient generalizing circuit under regularization. \citeonline{davies2023unifying} argue that grokking and double descent reflect a common pattern: learning simple patterns quickly and complex patterns slowly, leading to delayed improvements in generalization.

\textbf{Distribution shift.} A distribution shift is a change in the statistical properties of data between training and evaluation \cite{quinonero2022datashift}. Such shifts can degrade performance, but they can also create conditions where learning dynamics reveal structure only after prolonged training. \citeonline{Rabanser2019detect_shift} show that two-sample testing methods and domain-discriminating approaches can detect and characterize shifts, which motivates adapting shift-detection tools to quantify the training/test gaps that may trigger grokking.

\textbf{Implications for this thesis:} The literature motivates three commitments: (i) treat grokking as a data- and phase-dependent phenomenon rather than a property of a specific architecture; (ii) report training dynamics with phase-aware measurements; and (iii) explicitly characterize distribution shifts using statistical tests where possible, to relate delayed generalization to measurable properties of the data.

\section{Neuro-symbolic AI}
Neuro-symbolic AI spans a design space ranging from symbolic-first systems (logic programs calling learned perception modules) to neural-first systems regularized or evaluated by symbolic structure. Following Kautz's taxonomy, axes include the primary substrate (neural or symbolic), the coupling strength (tight vs.~loose), and the directionality of information flow (constraints, explanations, or supervision) \cite{kautz2022thirdaisummer,Garcez2009}.

This thesis positions itself as neural-first with loose but differentiable coupling: we learn neural representations (via SAEs) and apply soft logic validators that map activations to truth scores in $[0,1]$. A validator encodes a predicate or rule as a differentiable function of features (e.g., $p(s_\ell)\in[0,1]$), aggregates them with t-norms to express conjunction/disjunction, and produces a satisfaction score or penalty. Logic Tensor Networks (LTN) are an instance of such soft logic, supporting predicate grounding over tensors and differentiable fuzzy operators \cite{serafini2016ltn}.

Validators serve two roles: as training-time regularizers to favor representations that satisfy desired constraints and as evaluation-time metrics to quantify constraint satisfaction and coverage. In the cross-layer setting, validators can be shared across layers to test whether semantics are preserved by $T_{\ell\to k}$.

\subsection{Implications for this thesis.} We adopt soft logic validators (LTN-style) as both losses and metrics. They provide portable semantic checks across layers and yield signals for steering. We will report satisfaction rates and penalty magnitudes alongside reconstruction/alignment metrics to assess whether aligned features remain semantically meaningful.

\section*{Interpretability}
Interpretability goals vary along two axes: local vs.~global and mechanistic vs.~post-hoc. Local methods explain particular inputs or activations; global methods summarize model behavior or circuits. Mechanistic approaches aim to uncover causal or algorithmic structure (e.g., feature-level circuits), while post-hoc methods provide approximate explanations without claiming faithful mechanism \cite{doshi2017rigorous,jacovi2020faithfulness}.

Polysemanticity---multiple unrelated features entangled within a single dimension of the residual stream---motivates SAEs: by promoting sparsity and overcomplete bases, SAEs aim to separate conflated concepts into more interpretable features. However, SAEs must be evaluated for fidelity (reconstruction), usefulness (downstream control), and stability (consistency across seeds and epochs).

In this thesis, we use SAEs to obtain sparse features $s_\ell$ per layer, transcoders to align features across layers, and validators to attach semantic meaning to features. This triad supports both mechanistic inspection (feature alignment and validator transfer) and practical steering.

\subsection{Criteria for actionable mechanistic interpretability}
We evaluate interpretability by four criteria connected to downstream decisions. \textbf{Faithfulness} asks whether internal measurements track causal or algorithmic structure rather than plausible narratives; we operationalize it with intervention-aware checks and baseline comparisons \cite{jacovi2020faithfulness}. \textbf{Stability} asks whether features persist across seeds/checkpoints; we operationalize it with correspondence and drift metrics. \textbf{Transferability} asks whether local structures and validators remain meaningful across layers/variants; we operationalize it with transcoder alignment and validator consistency. \textbf{Actionability} asks whether explanations support controllable interventions; we operationalize it with steering-locality trade-off curves and collateral regression reporting.

\textit{Takeaway for this thesis:} interpretability claims are accepted only when faithfulness, stability, transferability, and actionability are jointly supported by reproducible metrics in Chapter~\ref{chpt:circuitry}.

\textbf{Implications for this thesis:} We prioritize mechanistic interpretability with practical utility: features should reconstruct well, be sparse and stable, align across layers, and satisfy reusable validators. These criteria drive the metrics and ablations used later.

\subsection{Sparse Auto-encoders}
A sparse auto-encoder on layer $\ell$ learns an encoder $E_\ell: h_\ell\mapsto s_\ell$ and a decoder $D_\ell: s_\ell\mapsto \hat h_\ell$ that reconstruct the residual stream from a sparse code. Sparsity encourages features that fire selectively, which can reduce polysemanticity and improve interpretability. Training typically minimizes reconstruction error with a sparsity-promoting penalty or target activation rate.

We report three primary metrics: (i) sparsity of $s_\ell$ (e.g., average activation rate), (ii) reconstruction error $\|h_\ell-\hat h_\ell\|$, and (iii) stability across seeds and epochs (feature correspondence and drift). Practical considerations include normalization of $h_\ell$, preventing dead features, and calibrating sparsity targets to balance fidelity and disentanglement.

\textbf{Implications for this thesis:} SAE outputs $s_\ell$ constitute the feature space for alignment and validation. Stability protocols (cross-seed/epoch matching) and sparsity/reconstruction trade-offs are part of all SAE reports and ablations.

\subsection{Cross-layer Transcoder}
As depth composes functions, semantically related signals can appear at multiple layers in transformed bases. We introduce a cross-layer transcoder $T_{\ell\to k}$ to map $s_\ell$ into the basis of layer $k$, yielding $\hat s_k$. Training objectives include alignment to $s_k$ (when available), semantic consistency under validators, and regularization to prevent degenerate solutions.

Evaluation covers prediction quality of $\hat s_k$ against $s_k$, transfer of validator satisfaction across layers, and stability across seeds. Care is taken to avoid the transcoder inventing semantics not present in $s_\ell$ or $s_k$ (checked via reconstruction and validator consistency tests).

\textbf{Implications for this thesis:} $T_{\ell\to k}$ enables cross-layer analysis and control. Success is defined by high alignment with $s_k$, stable mappings, and preserved validator semantics, which we quantify in Chapter~\ref{chpt:circuitry} and later.

\subsection{LTN Cross-layer Transcoder}
The LTN Cross-layer Transcoder combines layerwise SAEs, cross-layer mapping, and soft logic validators. SAEs produce sparse features per layer; $T_{\ell\to k}$ aligns these features; validators score whether features satisfy desired relations. These scores serve two functions: regularizing the transcoder to prefer semantically consistent alignments and producing signals for steering.

At a high level, the method aims to improve interpretability that is useful for control: aligned sparse features that reconstruct well, transfer across depth, and respect declarative constraints. Detailed objectives, training schedules, and steering surfaces are specified in Chapter~4; here we motivate the combination and its expected benefits.
The concrete LSNC protocol (predicate grounding with LoRA, composite scoring, and guided beam-search failure policies) is intentionally deferred to the methodology chapter to keep this background section conceptual.

\textbf{Implications for this thesis:} This section bridges Background to Methodology. We will (i) train SAEs per layer and report sparsity/reconstruction/stability, (ii) train $T_{\ell\to k}$ and report alignment and semantic transfer, and (iii) use LTN validators both as regularizers and evaluation metrics, ultimately yielding signals for steering.
